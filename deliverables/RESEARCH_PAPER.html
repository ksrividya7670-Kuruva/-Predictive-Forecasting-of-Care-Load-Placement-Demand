Executive Abstract

The Unaccompanied Alien Children (UAC) Program operates within one of the most volatile and humanitarian-critical operational environments in the federal government. Children entering federal care through U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) custody must, by statutory mandate under the Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act (TVPRA), be transferred to the custody of the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) within 72 hours. While historical descriptive dashboards quantify past shelter occupancy and border encounters, operational planners have historically lacked forward-looking predictive intelligence to forecast upcoming shelter bed demand, anticipate sponsor placement bottlenecks, and mobilize child-welfare staff in advance of capacity breaches.

This study presents a rigorous end-to-end predictive modeling architecture for forecasting daily HHS Care Load ($y^{\text{care}}_t$) and Discharge (Placement) Demand ($y^{\text{disch}}_t$) across short- (7-day), medium- (14-day), and strategic-horizon (30-day) operational planning windows. Utilizing an empirical dataset of 1,075 calendar days (January 2023 to December 2025) spanning 720 federal reporting cycles, we resolve systemic calendar reporting gaps via continuous daily reindexing and time-weighted interpolation. We establish that care load and flow series are integrated of order one ($I(1)$) and exhibit profound cross-pipeline lead-lag relationships, with CBP apprehensions leading HHS intake transfers by 24–48 hours ($r = 0.9256$ at lag 1).

We benchmark baseline models (Naïve Persistence, 7-day Moving Average), classical statistical formulations (Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing, Seasonal ARIMA), and recursive machine learning architectures (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) using strict temporal walk-forward backtesting across expanding-window folds. Empirical results establish SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,0,1)$_7$ as the superior statistical forecaster for active care load, achieving a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 40.56 children, a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 1.83%, and an overall Forecast Accuracy of 98.17%, closely followed by Gradient Boosting (MAE 46.46, MAPE 2.02%, Accuracy 97.98%). In placement demand forecasting, smoothed statistical methods maintain superior robustness against erratic weekend reporting artifacts. Finally, we formulate early-warning Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)—including Surge Lead Time, Capacity Breach Probability, and a 4-Tier Operational Readiness Action Protocol—translating algorithmic forecasts into actionable shelter bed allocations, caseworker-to-child ratios, and medical mobilization triggers.

Keywords: Unaccompanied Children (UAC), Care Load Forecasting, Placement Demand, Time-Series Analysis, SARIMA, Gradient Boosting, Capacity Breach Risk, Child Welfare Operations, Humanitarian Logistics.

1. Operational Background and Humanitarian Context

1.1 The Federal UAC Care Pipeline

The U.S. Unaccompanied Alien Children (UAC) Program represents an inter-agency humanitarian system governed by the Homeland Security Act of 2002 and the William Wilberforce Trafficking Victims Protection Reauthorization Act of 2008 (TVPRA, 8 U.S.C. § 1232). Under federal law: 1. Intake and Apprehension: Unaccompanied minors encountered at ports of entry or between border crossings are initially processed by CBP (Border Patrol or Office of Field Operations). 2. Statutory Transfer Window: CBP is legally mandated to transfer unaccompanied minors to HHS/ORR care within 72 hours of determining that the minor is an unaccompanied child, absent exceptional circumstances. 3. Custodial Care and Shelter: HHS/ORR accommodates children in a nationwide network of state-licensed shelters, group homes, transitional foster care, and temporary Influx Care Facilities (ICFs). 4. Discharge and Placement: HHS caseworkers conduct background vetting of potential sponsors (typically parents, close relatives, or approved guardians) to release children safely into community settings while their immigration proceedings continue.

1.2 The Operational Dilemma: Descriptive vs. Predictive Posture

Historically, federal child welfare administrators relied on descriptive analytics—retrospective reporting of how many children entered or exited custody yesterday. In a highly non-stationary environment subject to seasonal migration patterns, geopolitical shifts in Central America, and sudden border enforcement policy adjustments (such as Title 42 expiration and subsequent asylum processing rules), descriptive reporting inherently leads to lagged, reactive decision-making.

When sudden surges occur: - Border patrol holding stations become bottlenecked, threatening compliance with the 72-hour statutory deadline. - HHS state-licensed shelters rapidly hit 90%+ occupancy, forcing emergency activation of costly and contentious Influx Care Facilities (ICFs). - Staff-to-child ratios degrade, elevating caseworker burnout, clinical assessment delays, and child length of stay (LOS).

Conversely, over-procuring emergency shelter beds during sudden migration lulls wastes millions of taxpayer dollars in standby capacity fees. A reliable, forward-looking predictive engine is thus imperative to provide HHS and CBP with actionable lead time.

2. Mathematical Problem Formulation & Dynamic System Identity

2.1 The Dynamic Stock-Flow Care Load Identity

The physical dynamics of the HHS care system represent an open queuing network governed by a conservation-of-flow differential equation. In discrete daily time steps $t \in {1, 2, \dots, T}$:

$$S_t = S_{t-1} + I_t - E_t + \epsilon^{\text{recon}}_t$$

Where: - $S_t \equiv \text{hhs_in_care}t$ is the Stock variable representing active children in HHS care at the end of day $t$. - $I_t \equiv \text{transfers_to_hhs}_t$ is the Inflow variable representing children transferred from CBP custody into HHS facilities on day $t$. - $E_t \equiv \text{hhs_discharges}_t$ is the Outflow variable representing children successfully placed with vetted sponsors on day $t$. - $\Delta S_t = S_t - S{t-1}$ is the net daily change in care load. - $N_t = I_t - E_t$ represents the Net Flow (Care Pressure Indicator). - $\epsilon^{\text{recon}}_t$ is an administrative reconciliation noise term accounting for intraday database updates, age-outs (turning 18), or transfers to other specialized custodial authorities.

2.2 Objective Formulation

The primary forecasting objectives are dual-target, multi-horizon regressions:

  1. Care Load Trajectory Formulation: $$\hat{S}{t+h} = f_h\left(S{t}, S_{t-1}, \dots, I_{t}, I_{t-1}, \dots, E_{t}, E_{t-1}, \dots, \mathbf{X}^{\text{upstream}}_t, \mathbf{C}_t\right)$$ For horizons $h \in {1, 7, 14, 30}$ days, where $\mathbf{X}^{\text{upstream}}_t$ represents leading CBP apprehension and custody indicators, and $\mathbf{C}_t$ represents calendar embeddings.

  2. Placement Demand Formulation: $$\hat{E}{t+h} = g_h\left(E_t, E{t-1}, \dots, S_t, \mathbf{C}_t\right)$$ Predicting sponsor release throughput to identify whether downstream placement operations can offset anticipated upstream intake.

  3. Uncertainty Quantification: For each point forecast $\hat{y}{t+h}$, generate well-calibrated $1-\alpha$ confidence bounds $[\hat{L}{t+h}^{(\alpha)}, \hat{U}_{t+h}^{(\alpha)}]$ for $\alpha \in {0.20, 0.05}$.

4. Analytical Methodology: Data Preparation & Feature Engineering

4.1 Resolving Non-Reporting Days via Continuous Reindexing

Federal public reports for the UAC program are issued on a 5-day reporting cycle (Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday), with Friday publishing only intermittently (2 observations) and Saturday publishing zero reports. To establish a mathematically continuous daily lattice: 1. Reindexed the temporal index onto a strict daily frequency: $\mathcal{T} = {t_{\min}, t_{\min}+1, \dots, t_{\max}}$ where $\Delta t = 1 \text{ day}$. 2. Implemented continuous time-weighted linear interpolation across non-reporting gaps: $$S_t = S_{t_0} + \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 - t_0} (S_{t_1} - S_{t_0}) \quad \forall t \in (t_0, t_1)$$ For stock variables ($S_t$), this preserves physical mass continuity without introducing ungrounded polynomial oscillations.

4.2 Time-Series Decomposition (STL Architecture)

Using classical Seasonal-Trend Decomposition (STL) with additive specification and weekly period $m = 7$:

$$y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

  • Trend Component ($T_t$): Isolates the macro multi-year policy regimes, filtering out short-term fluctuations.
  • Seasonal Component ($S_t$): Isolates systematic 7-day administrative rhythms. Discharges drop significantly over weekends (averaging <10/day) and surge on Thursdays and Sundays (>200/day during high-volume periods) due to court scheduling and sponsor travel arrangements.
  • Residual Component ($R_t$): Captures high-frequency stochastic border encounter spikes and administrative data reconciliations.

4.3 Feature Engineering Matrix

To equip machine learning models with rich predictive representations, 61 distinct features were engineered without lookahead leakage:

  1. Autoregressive Lag Features:
  2. Targets: $S_{t-1}, S_{t-2}, S_{t-3}, S_{t-7}, S_{t-14}, S_{t-21}$
  3. Outflows: $E_{t-1}, E_{t-2}, E_{t-3}, E_{t-7}, E_{t-14}$
  4. Inflows: $I_{t-1}, I_{t-2}, I_{t-3}, I_{t-7}, I_{t-14}$
  5. Upstream Leading Indicators:
  6. Apprehensions & CBP Custody: $A_{t-1}, A_{t-2}, A_{t-3}, A_{t-7}$ and $C_{t-1}, C_{t-2}, C_{t-3}, C_{t-7}$
  7. Rolling Moving Windows & Volatility:
  8. Rolling Means: $\mu_{7}(S), \mu_{14}(S), \mu_{7}(E), \mu_{14}(E), \mu_{7}(I), \mu_{14}(I)$
  9. Rolling Volatility (Standard Deviation): $\sigma_{7}(S), \sigma_{14}(S), \sigma_{7}(A)$
  10. Flow-Based Dynamics:
  11. Net Care Pressure: $N_t = I_t - E_t$
  12. Cumulative Flow Pressure: $\sum_{k=0}^{6} N_{t-k}$ and $\sum_{k=0}^{13} N_{t-k}$
  13. Turnover Ratios: $\frac{I_t}{C_t + 1}$ and Discharge Rate $\frac{E_t}{S_t + 1}$
  14. Kinematic Indicators (Velocity & Acceleration):
  15. 7-Day Care Load Velocity: $v_7 = \frac{S_{t-1} - S_{t-8}}{7}$
  16. 14-Day Care Load Velocity: $v_{14} = \frac{S_{t-1} - S_{t-15}}{14}$
  17. Border Surge Z-Score: $Z^{\text{surge}}t = \frac{A{t-1} - \mu_{14}(A)}{\sigma_{14}(A)}$
  18. Calendar & Cyclical Embeddings:
  19. Day-of-week sine/cosine encodings: $\sin(2\pi \cdot \text{dow} / 7)$, $\cos(2\pi \cdot \text{dow} / 7)$
  20. Month sine/cosine encodings: $\sin(2\pi \cdot (\text{month}-1) / 12)$, $\cos(2\pi \cdot (\text{month}-1) / 12)$
  21. Weekend binary indicator: $\mathbb{I}(\text{dow} \in {5, 6})$

5.1 Baseline Models

  1. Naïve Persistence Forecaster: $$\hat{y}_{t+h} = y_t \quad \forall h \in {1, \dots, H}$$ Serves as the minimum benchmark. Under $I(1)$ random walk assumptions, persistence is notoriously competitive over ultra-short horizons ($h \le 3$).
  2. Moving Average (7-Day) Forecaster: $$\hat{y}{t+h} = \frac{1}{7} \sum{k=0}^6 y_{t-k}$$ Smooths short-term intra-week fluctuations.

5.2 Statistical Formulations

  1. Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing: Employs additive trend and additive weekly seasonality: $$\hat{y}{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h b_t + s{t + h - m(k+1)}$$ Where $\ell_t$ is level, $b_t$ is trend slope, and $s_t$ is the seasonal component ($m = 7$).
  2. Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA): Expressed in compact backshift operator notation as $\text{SARIMA}(p,d,q) \times (P,D,Q)_s$: $$\Phi_P(B^s) \phi_p(B) (1 - B)^d (1 - B^s)^D y_t = \Theta_Q(B^s) \theta_q(B) \epsilon_t$$ Parameterized with non-seasonal differencing $d = 1$, autoregressive lag $p = 1$, moving average $q = 1$, and seasonal order $(1, 0, 1)_7$ with weekly period $s = 7$.

5.3 Machine Learning Recursive Regressors

  1. Random Forest Regressor (RF): Non-linear ensemble of 80 randomized decision trees utilizing feature subsampling, max depth of 10, and bootstrap bagging.
  2. Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR): Sequential boosted decision trees trained with learning rate $\eta = 0.08$, max depth of 4, subsample ratio 0.85, minimizing squared error loss: $$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \gamma_m h_m(x)$$

Recursive Multi-Step Rollout:
For machine learning architectures, future projections across horizon $H$ are generated recursively: at each step $t+k$, the predicted value $\hat{y}_{t+k}$ is appended to the historical state vector, autoregressive lags and rolling windows are updated dynamically over the rolling window, and step $t+k+1$ is evaluated.

5.4 Uncertainty & Confidence Interval Formulation

Uncertainty intervals for statistical models are derived directly from the state-space Kalman filter forecast covariance. For machine learning and baseline models, intervals are computed via empirical residual standard errors scaled by the horizon diffusion factor $\sqrt{h}$:

$$\hat{y}{t+h} \pm z{\alpha/2} \cdot \hat{\sigma}_{\epsilon} \cdot \sqrt{h}$$

Where $z_{0.10} = 1.282$ for 80% confidence intervals, and $z_{0.025} = 1.960$ for 95% confidence intervals.

6. Experimental Setup & Multi-Horizon Walk-Forward Backtesting

6.1 Validation Design: Strict Temporal Walk-Forward

Random train-test splits or standard $K$-fold cross-validation cause catastrophic temporal data leakage in time-series forecasting. We implement an Expanding-Window Walk-Forward Validation protocol: - Initial training window: $T_{\text{train}} \ge 720$ continuous daily records. - Split size: $H_{\max} = 30$ days per test fold. - Evaluation folds: 4 sequential out-of-sample backtest windows. - In each fold, models are fit exclusively on data prior to cutoff date $t_{\text{split}}$, and recursively projected forward without updating model weights during the 30-day forecast window.

6.2 Evaluation Metrics

Let $y_i$ denote the ground truth and $\hat{y}_i$ denote the model prediction for $i = 1, \dots, N$:

  1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE): $$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$$
  2. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$$
  3. Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): $$\text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left| \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i} \right|$$
  4. Symmetric MAPE (sMAPE): $$\text{sMAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{|y_i - \hat{y}_i|}{(|y_i| + |\hat{y}_i|)/2}$$
  5. Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$): $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$
  6. Forecast Accuracy (%): $$\text{Accuracy} = \max\left(0, 100\% - \text{MAPE}\right)$$
  7. Forecast Stability Index (FSI): $$\text{FSI} = \sigma_{\text{MAE}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^K (\text{MAE}_k - \overline{\text{MAE}})^2}$$

8. Operational Early-Warning Architecture & Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

To transform raw numerical forecasts into proactive operational decisions, we define four core Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):

8.1 KPI Definitions & Mathematical Formulations

  1. Surge Lead Time (SLT): $$\text{SLT} = \min \left{ h \in {1, \dots, H} \mid \hat{S}_{t+h} \ge S^{\text{surge}} \right}$$ Measures the exact number of days of advance warning before projected care load breaches the surge mobilization threshold ($S^{\text{surge}} = 0.90 \times \text{Capacity}$). If no breach is projected within horizon $H$, $\text{SLT} = \infty$.
  2. Capacity Breach Probability (CBP): $$\text{CBP}H = \Pr\left( \max{1 \le h \le H} S_{t+h} \ge S^{\text{cap}} \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}t \right) \approx 0.6 \cdot \mathbb{I}(\max \hat{S}{t+h} \ge S^{\text{cap}}) + 0.4 \cdot \mathbb{I}(\max \hat{U}_{t+h}^{(0.05)} \ge S^{\text{cap}})$$ Combines point forecast breach with 95% upper confidence interval breach to capture tail risk.
  3. Forecast Stability Index (FSI): $$\text{FSI} = \sigma_{\text{MAE}(\text{folds})}$$ Measures consistency across distinct time periods. A lower FSI indicates the model maintains reliability during regime shifts.
  4. Projected Bed Utilization Rate ($U_H$): $$U_H = \frac{\max_{1 \le h \le H} \hat{S}_{t+h}}{S^{\text{cap}}} \times 100\%$$

10. Strategic Recommendations for HHS/ORR Leadership & Healthcare Planners

Based on our empirical and algorithmic findings, we formulate five strategic recommendations:

  1. Deploy SARIMA as Primary Operational Engine with ML Ensemble: Adopt SARIMA(1,1,1)(1,0,1)$_7$ as the primary automated forecasting engine for daily reporting, augmented by Gradient Boosting for 14-to-30 day strategic planning where non-linear upstream pipeline signals (CBP apprehensions) provide early warning.
  2. Establish Upstream Data Integration between CBP and HHS: Because CBP apprehensions lead HHS transfers with a 24-to-48 hour correlation of $r = 0.9256$, automated API integration of CBP apprehensions directly into the ORR predictive model provides 48 hours of advance warning before children arrive at shelter intake gates.
  3. Institutionalize the 4-Tier Operational Readiness Protocol: Codify the Tier 1–4 action matrix into ORR Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs). Mandate that Tier 2 triggers automatic contractor notifications and Tier 3 triggers warm standby of Influx Care Facilities.
  4. Implement Dynamic Weekend Sponsor Placement Workflows: Given that placement velocity drops sharply on weekends while border intake continues unabated, establish weekend skeleton staffing for final sponsor clearances to prevent weekly care load accumulation.
  5. Incorporate Real-Time Early-Warning Alerts in the Executive Dashboard: Deploy the developed Streamlit application within HHS internal networks to provide regional shelter coordinators and federal program managers with real-time capacity breach alerts and dynamic what-if simulation tools.

11. Conclusion

This project transitions the UAC program from historical descriptive monitoring to proactive, forward-looking predictive intelligence. By resolving calendar reporting gaps, establishing integration orders, and benchmarking baseline, statistical, and machine learning models through rigorous walk-forward validation, we demonstrate that future care load can be forecasted with over 98% accuracy up to 30 days in advance.

The integration of automated surge lead time indicators, capacity breach probabilities, and dynamic scenario simulators equips federal decision-makers with the tools needed to prevent shelter overcrowding, protect statutory transfer windows, optimize taxpayer expenditures, and uphold the humanitarian mission of the federal child welfare system.
